For most of its history, Bellwether Reservoir was treated as background infrastructure. The small city built the reservoir in 1958 by damming a shallow creek on its northern edge. It held drinking-water reserves, absorbed storm runoff, and offered a narrow belt of public land around the shore. By the 1990s, however, sediment had reduced the reservoir's useful capacity, algae blooms appeared during hot summers, and the concrete outlet gate required increasingly frequent repairs. Residents still used the perimeter trail, but sections flooded after heavy rain and two wooden footbridges had been closed.

The city began a formal review in 2019 after an inspection found that the outlet could remain safe with repairs but would not meet the city's expected needs during more intense storms. Engineers presented three broad options. The least expensive was to repair the gate and dredge only the deepest basin. A second option would dredge more widely, replace the outlet, and reinforce the existing dam. A third would lower the normal water level, create upstream wetlands, and rebuild the outlet so the reservoir could temporarily hold more stormwater. That third option preserved less open water but provided more habitat and the greatest modeled reduction in downstream flooding.

At first, public meetings split along familiar lines. Anglers and rowing-club members favored keeping as much open water as possible. People living downstream emphasized flood protection. Birders supported wetlands, while nearby residents worried that exposed mud and construction traffic would last for years. The parks department also warned that every option would require at least one long trail closure. Rather than select an option immediately, the council funded a ten-month design study and created a panel of sixteen residents, including representatives from each of those groups.

The panel asked the engineering team to test a hybrid. Under the revised plan, crews would remove sediment from the central basin but use some of it, after testing, to shape three wetland shelves along the northern shore. The normal water level would fall by about forty centimeters. A new adjustable outlet would let operators lower the reservoir before a forecast major storm, adding temporary storage without keeping the basin empty all season. The southern half would retain enough uninterrupted water for rowing, although the usable course would be shorter. Two trail segments would move uphill, and the closed footbridges would be replaced with a single longer crossing.

Cost estimates changed as the design became more specific. In 2021 dollars, the gate-repair option was estimated at $18 million, the reinforced-reservoir option at $31 million, and the original wetland option at $34 million. The hybrid was estimated at $36 million because it combined dredging, wetland construction, a new outlet, and trail work. Staff projected that grants for flood control and habitat could cover as much as $14 million of the hybrid, but those grants were competitive and could not be guaranteed. The council therefore approved design work in stages and required a new vote before the main construction contract.

Monitoring during design revealed another complication. Tests found low concentrations of industrial contaminants in sediment near an old storm sewer. Most dredged material could be reused on site, but roughly eight percent would need to be transported to a licensed disposal facility. That finding added an estimated $2.6 million and prompted the city to redesign truck routes away from two elementary schools. It also delayed the planned start from autumn 2022 to spring 2023.

Construction began in April 2023. The contractor first built a temporary bypass channel so creek water could move around the work zone. The reservoir was then lowered in stages rather than drained at once. This protected fish habitat in the southern basin and kept enough water available for emergency supply, but it made dredging slower. The perimeter trail closed completely for seven months. A signed detour added 1.8 kilometers to the usual route, and a free weekend shuttle connected the two ends for people who could not manage the longer walk.

The wetland shelves were planted in early 2024 with sedges, rushes, and shrubs selected for alternating wet and dry conditions. A dry spring forced the city to irrigate several sections, an expense the original construction budget had not included. Survival surveys that autumn found that 82 percent of the plantings had established. The contract had set a target of 80 percent, so the contractor did not need to replant entire shelves, though crews replaced several failed patches. Volunteers later added floating markers to keep paddlers out of the youngest vegetation.

The rebuilt reservoir reopened fully in June 2024. Its permanent open-water area was 17 percent smaller than before construction, and the rowing club shifted some practices to a regional lake. The restored trail was slightly longer but met modern accessibility grades, and the new bridge provided a continuous route around the shore for the first time in almost a decade. The adjustable outlet completed two test releases before storm season. Operators also adopted a written forecast protocol requiring agreement between the water and emergency-management departments before a pre-storm drawdown.

The first significant test came in March 2025, when a slow-moving storm dropped 112 millimeters of rain over three days. Operators lowered the reservoir by thirty centimeters before the heaviest rain. The basin then rose without overtopping, and gauges recorded a downstream peak flow 21 percent lower than the modeled flow under the old configuration. Engineers cautioned that a single storm could not prove the long-term reduction predicted by their models. They also noted that the ground had been relatively dry before the storm, which affected how much runoff reached the creek.

One year after reopening, the city reported mixed but generally favorable early results. Summer algae alerts fell from an average of six per year in the five years before construction to two in 2025. Staff counted nine nesting bird species on or near the wetland shelves, compared with five in the 2022 baseline survey. Trail counters showed six percent more visits than in the final full year before closure. Maintenance costs were higher than forecast because invasive plants appeared on one shelf and the adjustable gate required an additional software update.

The financial picture also remained unsettled. The final construction and disposal cost was $39.4 million, including the contaminant handling, irrigation, and a rise in concrete prices. Confirmed grants supplied $12.1 million, less than the maximum staff had once considered possible. The rest came from previously reserved utility funds and a twenty-year municipal bond. Annual debt payments began in 2025. The city publishes those payments alongside reservoir operating costs, but officials say at least five years of flood, water-quality, habitat, and maintenance data will be needed before they judge whether the hybrid delivered value proportional to its cost.

Debate has not disappeared. Some rowers argue that the city traded too much open water for benefits that remain partly modeled. Downstream residents point to the March storm and say the additional storage already mattered. Ecologists are encouraged by the nesting counts but want the city to measure insect and amphibian populations, not birds alone. The resident panel no longer meets monthly, yet eight members continue on an annual review group. Their first report asked the city to retain the operating protocol, publish raw gauge data, and compare actual maintenance spending with the forecasts made during design. The reservoir is no longer background infrastructure. It has become a visible experiment whose results will be measured over years.
